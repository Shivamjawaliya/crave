Driver Dispatching
& Restaurant → Rider Handover

How a nearby rider receives an order request, how many riders see it at once, and what happens at the restaurant counter when the food changes hands — explained in plain English, with block diagrams, and measured against what your code actually does today.

Self-contained HTML No external scripts Print / save as PDF Grounded in crave

The short version

  • Dispatching is a matchmaking problem with a timer. The system finds riders near the restaurant, offers the job to one of them, and gives them roughly 25–30 seconds to say yes.
  • Cascading (one rider at a time) is the industry default. Broadcasting to five riders at once feels faster but causes "fastest finger" frustration and is dangerous for people already riding.
  • The handover is a physical trust problem, not a technical one. A 4-digit PIN proves the rider who shows up is the rider who was assigned. The database transaction is the easy part.
  • Your repo has the handover UI but not the dispatch logic. Jobs are listed to every rider with no distance filter, the PIN has a hardcoded fallback (4821), and confirming pickup marks the order DELIVERED. Section 06 covers this in detail.

Read the diagram top to bottom. The first row is a continuous background process — it runs whether or not any order exists. The second row fires once per order. The third row is the actual offer, and it is the only part with a human decision in it.

Why the trigger is "food is nearly ready", not "order was placed"

If you dispatch the moment an order is placed, the rider sits at the restaurant for ten minutes while the kitchen cooks. They earn nothing for that time, the customer sees a rider standing still, and the rider learns to decline your orders. Dispatching when the food is READY_FOR_PICKUP — or a couple of minutes before, using average prep time — means the rider arrives roughly when the bag hits the counter.

02

How a rider sees the request

Three separate things have to be true before an order appears on a phone: the rider's position must be known, the food must be ready, and the rider must be idle.

Step 1 — The rider's phone keeps reporting its position

While the rider is "online", the driver app sends its latitude and longitude every 5–10 seconds. That is a heartbeat: a small, frequent message. The server stores it in a place designed for spatial queries, so "who is within 3 km of this restaurant?" is a fast lookup rather than a loop over every rider.

With Redis, this is two commands. GEOADD writes a rider's position into a sorted set. GEOSEARCH asks for everyone inside a radius. Redis also lets you sort the results by distance from a point, which is exactly what the dispatcher needs.

What counts as "available"

Position is only half of it. A rider standing outside the restaurant might be off-shift, already carrying two orders, or on a break. The dispatcher filters on availability too — usually an isAvailable flag plus a cap on active deliveries per rider. Position alone will happily offer a job to someone with four bags on the bike.

Step 2 — The kitchen marks the food ready

This is an internal event, not a user action on the rider's phone. The kitchen taps "Ready" on the KDS, the order service records that and publishes something like order.ready_for_pickup to the message broker, and the dispatch engine reacts by starting a round for that order.

The broker matters here for a practical reason: if the dispatch engine is restarting, the message waits in the queue. If you called the dispatch engine directly over HTTP and it was down, the order would simply never get a rider — silently.

Every element on this screen exists to answer one question fast: is this trip worth taking?

  • Pickup distance — how far they must ride to the food, unpaid or barely paid.
  • Drop distance — how long the paid leg is.
  • Earnings — the single number they optimise for. Show it plainly; hiding it trains riders to decline.
  • Timer — a countdown they can see. Without it, "expired" feels like a bug rather than a rule.

The countdown must be driven by the server, not the phone clock. A rider who changes their device time should not get an extra minute.

One detail that ruins the feature if missed

The offer screen must survive the rider's phone being locked or the app being backgrounded. That means a real push notification (FCM/APNs) with a high-priority channel, plus a ringing sound — not just a WebSocket message. A WebSocket only works while the app is open in the foreground. If you build the offer on WebSockets alone, riders will miss most of their orders and conclude the app is broken.

03

How many riders get the notification?

Two models. They differ in one decision: does one rider get a protected window, or do several race?

Cascading Recommended

Offer to the single best rider. Wait 25–30 seconds. If declined or expired, move to the next rider in the ranked list.

  • For: no racing, no false hope, safe for riders in motion.
  • For: you can explain to a rider exactly why they did or didn't get the order.
  • Against: if the first three riders all ignore their phone, assignment is slow.
Batch broadcast Faster, worse

Send to the top 3–5 riders at once. First to tap Accept wins.

  • For: very fast acceptance when plenty of riders are nearby.
  • Against: four people did the work of deciding and got nothing.
  • Against: encourages riders to tap while riding.

What "best rider" means

Cascading needs a ranking, or you are just picking a random neighbour. In practice it is a weighted score, not a single distance sort:

SignalWhy it mattersTypical weight
Distance to restaurant The dominant factor. Short pickup means faster delivery and a happier rider. High
Idle / on-shift A rider already mid-delivery cannot collect this order. Hard filter
Active delivery count Cap it. One or two concurrent orders is normal; five is a broken experience. Hard filter
Acceptance rate Riders who ignore offers get deprioritised over time. Medium
Rating Matters for customer experience, but should not starve new riders of work. Low
Vehicle type A bicycle cannot realistically take a 7 km order. Medium
Time since last offer Spreads work fairly; prevents one rider absorbing every order. Low–medium

The bug that always appears here

Two workers can process the same expiry at the same time and both offer the job to two different riders. Or a rider accepts at the same moment the timer fires. The fix is the same in both cases: the expiry and the acceptance must both be conditional writes — "update this row to ACCEPTED only if it is still OFFERED to me". Whichever write lands second updates zero rows and loses cleanly. Never read the row, decide, then write. Your current acceptJob does exactly the read-decide-write version; see section 06.

04

The Restaurant → Rider handover

The rider is at the counter and the food is in a bag. This is the moment where a wrong bag or a stolen order can happen, and no amount of good software fixes it unless the two people check something together.

🛵 Delivery partner
🍳 Kitchen staff
1 Arrives at the restaurant and taps "Arrived" in the app.
2 The KDS now shows "Marcus is at the counter" on that order's card.
3 Shows the phone screen: order number #ORD-1234 and a 4-digit pickup code.
4 Matches the number against the bag label on the shelf.
5 Reads out the code, e.g. 4 8 2 1.
6 Types the code into the KDS and confirms the handover.
7 Server validates the code, writes the status change, and the customer's app buzzes:
"Your food is on the way."

Why a PIN at all?

The order number on the rider's screen is visible to anyone who glances at it. The PIN is not. It proves two things at once: that this rider is the one the system assigned, and that the staff member handed the bag to a real person rather than leaving it on a shelf. It also creates a record — "collected at 8:14pm by rider X, verified by staff Y" — which is what you need when a customer says the food never arrived.

The same idea appears at the other end of the trip: the customer gives the rider a delivery PIN, or the rider photographs the drop. Both exist for the same reason — a moment where a physical handover needs a digital witness.

What the database should record

The handover is one transaction. It is not a status update plus a log plus a notification sent separately — if any of those fail, you end up with an order that says "picked up" and a customer who was never told.

WriteWhat it captures
order.status = PICKED_UPThe order has left the restaurant.
delivery.status = PICKED_UP, picked_up_atWhich rider holds it and when.
Status history rowWho changed it (RESTAURANT), from what, to what, when — the audit trail.
Outbox rowOrderPickedUp, to be published to the broker by a relay.

The outbox is the one piece people skip and later regret. If you publish to RabbitMQ after committing, and the publish fails, the event is gone and nothing retries it. If you publish inside the transaction and the transaction rolls back, you have told the customer about a pickup that did not happen. Writing the event into a table in the same transaction, and letting a small poller publish it afterwards, solves both.

Two extra handover states worth having

  • At store. The rider arrives but the food is not ready. Without this state, the rider has no button to press and the kitchen has no signal that a human is waiting.
  • Handover failed. Wrong PIN, wrong bag, food cold, rider refuses. This needs a real outcome — not a silent timer — because someone has to decide whether to remake the order or refund it.
05

Technologies, and what each one actually buys you

The list is conventional. What matters is why each piece is there — most of them can be replaced, but not with nothing.

StepJob to be doneTypical toolWhy that tool
1. GPS tracking Answer "who is near this restaurant?" in milliseconds Redis Geospatial Purpose-built radius and distance queries. Postgres with PostGIS also works; plain lat/lng columns do not scale to radius search.
2. Dispatch trigger Start a round exactly when food is ready, without losing the signal if a service restarts RabbitMQ topic exchange Durable queues mean a restarted dispatcher still sees the "ready" message.
3. Ranking Pick the best rider from the eligible set Node.js / Go service Ordinary application logic. No special tooling needed — it is arithmetic plus a database read.
4. Offer delivery Make a phone ring even when the app is closed FCM / APNs + WebSocket Push wakes a backgrounded phone; sockets update the screen once it is open. You need both.
5. Acceptance Ensure exactly one rider wins the job Postgres conditional update A transaction plus a version/status guard. The database is the arbiter, not the app.
6. Handover verification Prove the right rider took the right bag PIN in KDS, server-side check Simple, works offline-ish, no hardware. QR scanning is a nice upgrade, not a requirement.
A caution about the tools

Every row in that table can be replaced by something simpler: Postgres instead of Redis, polling instead of push, a database transaction instead of a broker. What cannot be removed is the requirement — you still need a fast proximity query, a reliable offer, exactly-one acceptance, and a verifiable handover. Choose the simplest tool that satisfies the requirement, and only upgrade when you have a measurement that says the simple one fails.

06

What your repo does today

This is measured against the code, not the diagram above. Several of the pieces are genuinely there; several of the diagrams above are still aspirations.

What exists and works

  • Delivery and location models. Delivery with UNASSIGNED → ASSIGNED → ACCEPTED → PICKED_UP → IN_TRANSIT → DELIVERED, and DeliveryLocation for GPS points.
  • Order status enum. Includes READY_FOR_PICKUP, PICKED_UP, OUT_FOR_DELIVERY — the states the handover needs.
  • Realtime plumbing. The gateway consumes food_events with binding order.# and pushes updates to clients. The order service publishes order.<status> on every transition.
  • KDS PIN entry UI. A modal with code entry and an error state — the interface half of the handover.
  • Delivery endpoints. GET /delivery/jobs, POST /jobs/:id/accept, PATCH /jobs/:id/status, POST /jobs/:id/location.

The two that will bite hardest

1. The universal PIN. '4821' bypasses verification entirely, so the handover check currently proves nothing. Anyone who reads the code can collect any order.

2. Pickup marking as delivered. Confirming the handover sets the order to DELIVERED, which means the delivery leg is skipped and the customer is told their food arrived before it left the restaurant.

A design smell worth fixing early

Two services now write order.status: the order service through its validated updateStatus, and the delivery service directly. Once more than one place owns a field, the transition rules stop being a rule. The delivery service should ask the order service to make the change, or publish an event the order service consumes — not write the column itself.

07

What to build, in what order

Each step is useful on its own and does not depend on the next one. Do them in this order and you always have something that works.

  1. Fix the handover before building dispatching. Generate a real per-order PIN when the order is created, store it, validate it server-side, and change the success path to move the order to PICKED_UP. This is small, it closes a live correctness hole, and it makes the KDS honest. Everything else is an optimisation on top.
  2. Make acceptance atomic. Replace the read-check-write in acceptJob with a single conditional update guarded on status = UNASSIGNED, inside the transaction. Treat zero updated rows as "another rider got it". Two riders, one winner — without a lock.
  3. Give order status a single owner. Stop writing order.status from the delivery service. Either call the order service or publish an event it consumes, so every transition goes through isValidOrderTransition and every change is published to the broker.
  4. Add the outbox. One table, one poller, for order status events. Without it, a broker hiccup means a customer never learns their food is coming.
  5. Then build proximity. Start with PostGIS or a bounding-box query in Postgres and a proper "latest position per rider" table — you may never need Redis. Prune old location rows; keep only the latest position plus trip history.
  6. Only now build the cascading offer. An offers table with OFFERED / ACCEPTED / DECLINED / EXPIRED, a server-side expiry job, and a notifier that pushes to one rider at a time. This is the last piece because it is the one that depends on everything above.
The sanity check

At every step, ask: if the deliverable of this step were the only thing I shipped, would the system still be correct? Steps 1–4 each say yes. If a step only makes sense together with the next one, you have split it in the wrong place.
